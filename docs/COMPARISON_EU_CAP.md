# Two satellite systems for farm policy: YES-TECH in India and the EU's Area Monitoring System

*First draft. The points marked "to check" still need the regulation or scheme text in front of me.*

## Why compare them

Both India and the EU now use Sentinel data to make decisions about money paid to farmers. Both
run into the same physical limits I measured on my seven fields: small plots and cloudy seasons.
The comparison asks what each system uses the satellite *for*, and what happens when the
satellite cannot see.

## The two systems

**India, PMFBY with YES-TECH.** Under the national crop insurance scheme, technology-based yield
estimation began with paddy and wheat in Kharif 2023 and was extended to soybean from Kharif 2024,
with 30% weightage in the final yield to come from YES-TECH (Lok Sabha Unstarred Question 383,
22 July 2025). In Maharashtra, half of the cotton yield is to come from technology-based estimates
(Agrowon, 11 May 2025). The rest comes from crop-cutting experiments. Separately, the crop each
farmer has sown is recorded plot by plot with a geotagged photo through e-Peek Pahani.

**EU, the Area Monitoring System (AMS) under the Common Agricultural Policy.** Since 2023 every
member state has had to run an area monitoring system as part of its integrated administration
and control system (Regulation (EU) 2021/2116; detailed rules in Implementing Regulation (EU)
2022/1173, to check article numbers). Sentinel data are used to observe every declared parcel
through the season and check whether the conditions for area-based payments were met: for
example, that the declared crop is there, or that grassland was mown or the land was worked.

## Side by side

| | India: PMFBY / YES-TECH | EU: CAP Area Monitoring System |
|---|---|---|
| What the satellite is asked | How much yield was lost? A quantity | Did the farmer do what was declared? Mostly yes or no |
| Money at stake | An insurance payout after a loss | Eligibility for a direct payment |
| When the answer is needed | In the weeks that decide yield, which for kharif are the cloudiest | Any time before a deadline; a later clear pass can still answer |
| If the signal is unclear | A modelled estimate is still produced (to check how gaps are filled) | The parcel is followed up: a geotagged photo from the farmer or a field visit |
| Farmer's own evidence | Geotagged photo for crop recording (e-Peek Pahani); not, as far as I have read, for yield | A geotagged photo can settle a case the satellite could not (to check: which member states use it, and for which conditions) |
| Small plots | Most Vidarbha holdings are under 1 ha; my smallest field has 10 whole pixels at 10 m | Parcels too small or narrow for a reliable signal are handled by other means (to check: the JRC guidance) |

## What this says for my fields

The two systems use the same satellites for different tasks. The EU asks a question that can
wait for a clear day, and when the satellite cannot answer it, the burden of proof passes to a
photo or a visit. YES-TECH asks a question whose answer lies in the weeks the satellite is most
likely to miss. On my fields that was 65 to 75 days in July, August and September. So the same
cloud is a delay in one system and a gap in the evidence in the other, and in the second case it
is the farmer who carries the risk.

The EU design suggests one cheap step: a clear way for a farmer's own dated, geotagged photos to
count where the satellite record has a long gap. Farmers in Maharashtra already take such photos
for e-Peek Pahani.

## What I am not claiming

I have not seen how YES-TECH is computed in Wardha, and I have not studied how any one EU member
state runs its AMS. This is a comparison of what each system asks the satellite to do, using
public documents, not an evaluation of either system.
